<TextBlock label="Snapshot">
  Loveholidays was evaluating how to evolve its search and discovery experience with AI. Before building anything, we needed to understand how real package holiday consumers were already using AI tools in their planning — what they trusted, what frustrated them, and where AI helped versus where it got in the way.

  | | |
  |---|---|
  | **Company** | loveholidays |
  | **My role** | Product Design Manager — research co-facilitator and observer |
  | **Researcher** | Maria Narraway, Lead User Researcher |
  | **Method** | Qualitative usability sessions: live platform walkthroughs, behavioural observation, attitudinal interviews |
  | **Timeframe** | July – August 2025 |
  | **Participants** | 7 package holiday consumers (1 pilot + 6 full sessions) |

  **The brief:** Evaluate how users discover and plan short city breaks with AI travel tools. Understand the attitudinal and behavioural gap between where AI search is today and what users actually need from it.

  **The platforms tested:** loveholidays (structured search with keyword filters) and Mindtrip (freeform conversational AI for travel planning).
</TextBlock>

<TextBlock label="Research Goals">
  Three questions framed the study:

  1. **Attitudinal and behavioural patterns** — How are users already combining traditional and AI-powered tools in their planning? What habits and mental models are they bringing into new interfaces?
  2. **Trust and booking confidence** — How much do users rely on AI recommendations when making real booking decisions? What erodes or builds that trust?
  3. **Comparative performance** — Where does loveholidays' structured experience hold up against conversational AI, and where does it fall short?
</TextBlock>

<TextBlock label="Who We Spoke To">
  Seven package holiday consumers. A mix of desktop and mobile users, household incomes ranging from £20k to £90k+, across employment statuses including full-time, homemaker, and self-employed. All were actively considering a holiday and had prior experience booking package deals.

  5 of the 7 had already used AI tools — primarily ChatGPT or Gemini — for travel-related tasks. They weren't coming to this cold.

  Sessions ran approximately 60 minutes: a warm-up on current planning habits, a live platform walkthrough with both sites, structured preference questions, and a closing exercise imagining travel booking in five years.

  I was present as an observer on every session. Maria Narraway led facilitation.
</TextBlock>

<TextBlock label="Theme 1" heading="Fatigue, Curation, and Choice Reduction">
  The volume of choice in package holiday search is the single biggest source of frustration. Participants described the research process as long, manual, and emotionally draining. Several described a "give up" mindset — compromising on originally non-negotiable criteria just to get out of the loop.

  This isn't a filter problem. It's a curation problem. Users don't want to see everything available. They want to see the right things, quickly.

  AI's most compelling value proposition isn't breadth — it's precision. "Find my perfect match" is a fundamentally different product promise than "show me everything." The appetite for that promise is real. What's missing is an experience that delivers on it reliably.

  **Opportunity:** Invest in curation-first AI design. Fewer, better-matched results — with confidence signals about why they were surfaced — outperform exhaustive inventories. Two-way conversation design, where AI asks clarifying questions rather than returning a single list, directly addresses this.
</TextBlock>

<TextBlock label="Theme 2" heading="Familiarity vs. Novelty">
  5 of 7 participants preferred loveholidays' structured input over Mindtrip's freeform AI chat when planning a package holiday. The reason wasn't distrust of AI — it was control. Structured inputs showed results transparently. Freeform prompts felt like a black box.

  Mindtrip was widely described as "the future" — impressive in concept, but frustrating in use. Usability failures compounded quickly: itineraries appeared when users expected hotel lists; ATOL protection wasn't surfaced; it wasn't clear how results were generated or whether they were bookable.

  A separate observation: 5 of 6 participants didn't spontaneously use loveholidays' keyword filter feature during unprompted site interaction. When prompted, half weren't confident the results were accurate. Feature placement and trust both need addressing.

  **Opportunity:** Bridge traditional and AI interfaces rather than replacing one with the other. Integrate selection-based input alongside freeform prompting. Provide guided prompts and templates — a blank text field is cognitively harder than a structured form. New AI patterns demand intentional onboarding.
</TextBlock>

<TextBlock label="Theme 3" heading="Visuals and Interaction Patterns">
  Visual content isn't a nice-to-have in travel. It's how users make decisions. Participants described imagery — particularly hotel photography — as fundamental to even a superficial first assessment of a property. When AI tools returned text-heavy results, users disengaged.

  Mindtrip's text-first output was the most consistent source of frustration across the study. One participant described it directly: "I'd like something more visual — the image, the grid, the summary of the trip."

  Maps resonated strongly, particularly on desktop. Distance information surfaced contextually — "0.3km from the city centre" — was immediately appreciated and described as "really handy." Spatial context does a job that lists can't.
</TextBlock>

<TextBlock>
  **Opportunity:** Visual AI isn't a design polish decision — it's a purchase intent driver. The market opportunity is to lead with rich imagery inside conversational interfaces, not to bolt it on after. Proximity information, map-integrated results, and image-first grids are the gaps Mindtrip left open.
</TextBlock>

<TextBlock label="Theme 4" heading="Control, Transparency & Confidence">
  Users trust systems they can read. When filters are applied and results update in real time, confidence goes up. When AI generates a list with no visible logic, confidence collapses.

  Two moments surfaced this clearly. One participant questioned whether AI search results were commercially neutral — "Is ChatGPT weighting my thought process to one hotel over another? Has somebody paid a promotional price to get that at the top?" That's not cynicism. It's a reasonable question that the interface gave them no way to answer.

  A second participant noticed immediately that Mindtrip didn't display ATOL protection for package holidays. A single missing piece of familiar trust infrastructure made them hesitant to proceed. The absence of the expected, not just the presence of the unfamiliar, undermines confidence.

  Accuracy warnings — "results may not be accurate" — were particularly damaging. Disclaimers like this destroy the premise of using AI in the first place.

  **Opportunity:** Invest in an accuracy-first approach before marketing AI features broadly. Show reasoning where possible. Surface familiar trust signals (ATOL, Tripadvisor scores, pricing transparency) inside AI interfaces. "Trust us" is not a product strategy.
</TextBlock>

<TextBlock label="Theme 5" heading="Input Methods & Prompt Behaviour">
  Participants varied significantly in how they approached AI prompting — not based on tech familiarity, but on mental model.

  Two clear patterns emerged. Some users wrote compressed, form-like prompts: "Barcelona, 3 nights, September, £200pp, 1km to city centre." Others used natural, conversational language: "I want a 4-day holiday to Barcelona, staying by the beach, 4-star or above." A third group used iterative follow-ups — asking "why are there no all-inclusives in Barcelona?" — treating AI like a domain expert, not a search engine.

  The physical effort of typing and the cognitive effort of knowing what to type are both real barriers. One participant defaulted to established filter-based search explicitly because "ticking boxes is less effort." Another, with a cleft palate, found that voice recognition tools failed to understand them — forcing repeated input and creating a frustrating, exclusionary experience.

  **Opportunity:** The AI interface needs to handle all three prompt styles equally well — compressed shorthand, conversational sentences, and iterative clarifying questions. Voice recognition investment must include dialect and speech variation testing. Guided input templates reduce the cognitive load for users who don't know where to start.
</TextBlock>

<TextBlock label="What the Research Told Us" heading="Four principles for AI-powered travel search">
  These aren't aspirational values. They're design constraints that the research surfaced as non-negotiable:

  1. **Start with the familiar, enhance with AI.** Users expect standard inputs — destination, dates, travellers — before advanced features. AI that replaces the familiar creates friction. AI that enhances it builds trust.
  2. **Mimic the human travel agent, digitally.** Step-by-step, two-way conversations feel natural. Single-response AI that doesn't ask follow-ups feels broken. Memory and contextual reasoning — "of the hotels you just showed me, which is closest to the beach?" — are expected, not impressive.
  3. **Lead with visuals.** Balance depth and simplicity. Users need enough to decide, not everything available. Images, maps, and proximity signals are load-bearing, not decorative.
  4. **Curate, don't overwhelm.** The win isn't showing more. It's showing less, with higher confidence. Trusted, tailored results that feel made for the user outperform exhaustive inventories in both satisfaction and conversion intent.
</TextBlock>

<TextBlock label="Reflection" heading="What this study tells us about timing">
  This is a transitional period. Participants who had built comfort with AI tools over months approached both platforms very differently from those encountering conversational travel AI for the first time. There is no single "user" to design for right now.

  The safest route is not one search method. It's multiple pathways — structured filters, guided prompts, freeform text, voice — with clear onboarding that helps users get the most from whichever method they choose. Hedge the bets, not against AI, but against the assumption that users are ready to abandon everything familiar.

  The appetite is genuinely there. Multiple participants said, unprompted, they'd use AI over filters if it worked reliably. "If it can cut down the amount of time I spend clicking through pages, then I'd definitely use it." The conditional clause is the brief.

  **What's still unresolved:** This was 7 participants. It's directional, not statistically significant. A broader study — particularly one that maps where the wider loveholidays user base sits on the AI familiarity curve — would let us design for the distribution, not the outliers. That investment is the logical next step.
</TextBlock>
